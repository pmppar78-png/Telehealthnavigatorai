Short answer: a clinician generally has to be allowed to practice in the state where you are physically located during the visit, wherever the clinician sits. Federal telehealth guidance lists the ways that can happen: a full license in your state, a temporary practice law, a border-state exception, a licensure compact, or a telehealth registration. The compacts that matter most for mental health are compared in the table below.

One of the trickiest aspects of telehealth is that healthcare licensing is state-based, meaning your therapist or doctor may not be able to treat you if you move or travel to a different state. This guide explains how state licensing affects telehealth, what options exist for cross-state care, and how to maintain continuity in your treatment.

The Basic Rule: Patient Location Matters

The fundamental principle of healthcare licensing in the United States is governed by individual states. Review telehealth laws in each state for specific requirements.

Healthcare providers must be licensed in the state where the patient is physically located at the time of the visit.

This means:

  • If you live in Texas but your therapist is licensed only in California, they generally cannot treat you
  • If you travel from New York to Florida for vacation, your New York-licensed provider may not be able to conduct sessions while you're there
  • The provider's location doesn't matter – it's where YOU are that counts

Why These Rules Exist

State licensing requirements serve important purposes:

  • Consumer protection: Holds providers to each state's own standards
  • Accountability: Gives patients a clear path for complaints and discipline
  • Emergency response: Providers know local resources and can coordinate local care
  • Legal jurisdiction: Clear framework for malpractice and liability
  • State autonomy: States regulate healthcare within their borders

Interstate Licensing Compacts

Interstate compacts are agreements between states that allow licensed providers to practice across state lines more easily. These compacts are expanding access to telehealth:

PSYPACT (Psychology Interjurisdictional Compact)

Allows licensed psychologists to practice telepsychology across participating states.

  • Member states are listed on the official PSYPACT map
  • Psychologists can see patients in any member state
  • Telepsychology requires an Authority to Practice Interjurisdictional Telepsychology (APIT), which in turn requires ASPPB’s E.Passport
  • Also covers limited in-person practice through a Temporary Authorization to Practice (TAP)

Counseling Compact

Allows licensed professional counselors (LPCs) to practice across member states.

  • Live for licensees in 11 states as of October 29, 2026, Missouri the most recent
  • Covers telehealth and temporary in-person practice
  • Requires privilege to practice in member states

Social Work Licensure Compact

Newer compact for licensed clinical social workers (LCSWs).

  • Allows practice in member states without additional licenses
  • Activated, but multistate licenses are not yet being issued

Interstate Medical Licensure Compact (IMLC)

Streamlines licensing for physicians; 39 states, DC and Guam can act as a physician’s state of principal license.

  • Expedited pathway to obtain additional state licenses
  • Providers still hold individual state licenses
  • Useful for telehealth psychiatrists and other medical doctors

Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC)

Allows nurses to practice in any compact state with one multistate license.

  • 43 participating jurisdictions
  • Includes RNs and LPNs
  • Useful for telehealth nursing services

Common Scenarios and Solutions

Traveling for Vacation or Work

If you'll be in another state temporarily:

  • Inform your provider about your travel dates and locations
  • Ask if they're licensed in the state you're visiting (some providers hold multiple licenses)
  • Check if your provider participates in an interstate compact
  • Consider rescheduling sessions for before/after your trip
  • Some states allow brief continuity of care exceptions – ask your provider

College Students

Students may spend time in multiple states:

  • Determine your "primary" state (often your school's state during the academic year)
  • Ask if your provider is licensed in both your home state and school state
  • Use a platform with broad state coverage for flexibility
  • Consider your school's counseling center for on-campus support

Moving to a New State

If you're relocating permanently, our guide on transitioning your care can help you plan the move.

  • Give your current provider advance notice
  • Ask for referrals to providers in your new state
  • Request your treatment records to share with your new provider
  • Some providers hold licenses in multiple states – ask if they can continue treating you
  • Plan transition sessions to wrap up current treatment and prepare for the change

Snowbirds and Part-Year Residents

If you split time between states:

  • Find a provider licensed in both states (some hold multiple licenses)
  • Use a national platform that can match you with providers in each location
  • Consider two providers – one for each location – with coordination between them
  • Keep records of your treatment to share with providers in each location

Special Considerations for Medications

If you receive medication management through telehealth:

  • Prescriptions: The prescriber must be licensed where you are when the prescription is written
  • Controlled substances: Additional restrictions may apply for ADHD medications, anxiety medications, etc.
  • Pharmacy: Prescriptions can typically be filled at any pharmacy, but insurance coverage may vary
  • Refills: Plan ahead for travel – request refills before leaving your home state

Maintaining Continuity of Care

Tips for keeping your treatment on track despite state line challenges. If you are considering care outside the US, see our guide on international telehealth options.

  • Communicate proactively: Tell your provider about any upcoming travel or moves
  • Keep records: Maintain copies of your treatment history, diagnoses, and medication lists
  • Build a network: If you split time between states, establish care in each location
  • Use technology: Apps for mood tracking, medication reminders, and journaling don't have state restrictions
  • Emergency planning: Know local crisis resources wherever you are

The Future of Interstate Telehealth

Things are getting better on several fronts:

  • More compact states: Interstate compacts continue to add member states
  • Federal legislation: Ongoing discussions about federal telehealth licensing
  • Pandemic lessons: Many states permanently relaxed cross-state telehealth restrictions
  • Technology solutions: Platforms increasingly handle licensing complexity for you

Questions to Ask Your Provider

Before starting telehealth, ask:

  • In which states are you licensed to practice?
  • Do you participate in any interstate licensing compacts?
  • What happens to my care if I travel out of state?
  • Can you provide referrals if I need to transition to a new provider?
  • How do you handle prescription refills if I'm traveling?

Compacts are not a national license

PSYPACT lets a qualifying psychologist practice telepsychology into another effective PSYPACT state. It does not cover counselors, social workers, or prescribers. The state-by-state table is on state telehealth resources. Counselors have a separate Counseling Compact. Check the current map at counselingcompact.gov before you assume a counselor licensed at home can see you after you cross a state line. Both your location and the clinician's authority have to match on the day of the visit.

What if you are traveling?

Tell the clinician which state you will be sitting in. If they are not authorized there, move the appointment or see someone who is. A vacation week is not a reason for them to ignore the license. Crisis lines are the exception that does not depend on that license: call or text 988 anywhere in the United States.

Who runs into these rules

College students with a therapist back home, snowbirds who spend winters in Florida or Arizona, remote workers on long trips, military families, and anyone who has just moved all run into them. The rule follows where you are sitting during the session, not where you live or where your insurance card was issued.

How licensing is checked in practice

Most platforms ask for your location at sign-up and at each visit, and some read it from your device. A psychologist who holds a PSYPACT credential can see you temporarily in another participating state. Counselors, social workers and prescribers need a license in your state or a compact that covers their profession. Your insurer may also limit coverage to in-network providers in your home state.

Risks of ignoring the rule

  • The clinician can cancel the session or end care once they learn where you are, which can leave you without support at a bad moment.
  • Your insurer can deny a claim for an out-of-state visit.
  • Controlled-substance prescriptions also follow state rules, so a stimulant refill may not be legal to issue to a state where the prescriber isn't licensed.

What the primary sources say

Telehealth.HHS.gov lists five ways a provider may work across state lines: a full license, temporary practice laws, licensure reciprocity, multistate compacts and telehealth registration (Telehealth.HHS.gov, Licensing across state lines). It says some states' temporary practice laws exist for patients who move often, such as college students or seasonal residents, and that providers should verify the patient's location before each appointment.

Talk to a licensed clinician: If you're moving or traveling, tell your licensed clinician early so they can confirm what they're allowed to do and help you transition if needed. The site's free AI guide can help you sort through questions, but it is not a therapist or doctor.

Frequently asked questions

Can I see my telehealth provider if I travel to another state?

It depends on whether your provider is licensed in the state you're visiting. Check if your provider holds multiple state licenses or participates in an interstate compact that covers your destination.

What are interstate licensing compacts for telehealth?

Interstate compacts are agreements between states that allow licensed providers to practice telehealth across state lines more easily. Key compacts include PSYPACT for psychologists, the Counseling Compact for licensed professional counselors, the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact for physicians, and the Nurse Licensure Compact for nurses.

What happens to my telehealth care if I move to a new state?

When relocating permanently, give your current provider advance notice and ask for referrals to providers in your new state. Request your treatment records to share with your new provider, and check if your current provider holds licenses in multiple states that might include your new location.

Can I get prescriptions filled if I'm traveling out of state?

Prescriptions can typically be filled at any pharmacy nationwide, but the prescriber must be licensed in the state where you are when the prescription is written. For controlled substances, additional restrictions may apply. It's best to plan ahead and request refills before traveling.

How do telehealth platforms handle state licensing requirements?

Reputable telehealth platforms like BetterHelp, Talkspace, and Teladoc handle interstate licensing by maintaining large networks of providers licensed in multiple states. They automatically match you with therapists or doctors who are licensed in your current state.

What should college students know about telehealth across state lines?

College students often split time between their home state and school state. Ask if your provider is licensed in both locations, use a platform with broad state coverage for flexibility, and consider your school's counseling center for additional on-campus support during the academic year.
